🦅 The Comparative Adaptor — Avian Digestive Systems

This station explores how digestive anatomy is shaped by evolutionary diet adaptation. Compare the GI tract of a carnivorous raptor (Hawk) — optimized for rapid meat processing — with a granivorous passerine (Sparrow) — equipped with specialized structures for grinding hard seeds. Attach the correct modules and test food compatibility.

Biological Background — Avian Digestive Anatomy

Proventriculus: All birds have a glandular stomach (proventriculus) that secretes pepsin and HCl. This is analogous to the mammalian stomach.

Gizzard (Ventriculus): The gizzard is a muscular organ unique to birds (and some other vertebrates). In granivores like sparrows, it is extremely well-developed (thick muscular walls) and contains gastroliths (swallowed grit/pebbles) that grind hard seeds — replacing the function of teeth. In carnivores like hawks, the gizzard is less developed as soft animal tissue doesn’t require mechanical grinding.

Crop (Ingluvies): Many birds have a crop — a diverticulum of the esophagus for temporary food storage. Particularly well-developed in seed-eating birds (allows rapid ingestion, digest later in safety). Raptors typically have a simpler, smaller crop.

Intestinal Length: Herbivorous/granivorous birds have longer intestines relative to body size to allow more fermentation time for cellulose/starch digestion. Carnivores have shorter tracts as protein-rich animal tissue is more readily digested.
