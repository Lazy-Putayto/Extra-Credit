💊 The Vitamin Solubility Filter

This station models the differential absorption and storage of vitamins based on their solubility properties. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) dissolve in lipids and are stored in adipose tissue and the liver. Water-soluble vitamins (B complex, C) dissolve in aqueous solution and are rapidly excreted in urine when consumed in excess.

Biological Background — Vitamin Classification

Fat-Soluble Vitamins (A, D, E, K): These vitamins possess long hydrophobic carbon chains or ring structures. They are absorbed together with dietary fats via micelle formation and chylomicron packaging in the small intestine, entering the lymphatic system before reaching the bloodstream. They accumulate in the liver and adipose tissue over time.

Water-Soluble Vitamins (B complex, C): These vitamins have polar hydroxyl or carboxyl functional groups making them hydrophilic. They dissolve directly in the aqueous intestinal contents and are absorbed via specific carrier proteins. Once plasma concentrations exceed the renal threshold, they are filtered by the glomerulus and excreted in urine.

Clinical Significance — Toxicity Risk

Because fat-soluble vitamins accumulate in the body, excessive intake can cause hypervitaminosis. For example, Vitamin A toxicity (hypervitaminosis A) causes intracranial hypertension and liver damage. Vitamin D excess causes hypercalcemia. Water-soluble vitamins are generally safer in excess (except B6, which can cause peripheral neuropathy at very high doses).
