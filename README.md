
The Metabolic Maze Website is an interactive digital learning platform developed for General Biology 2 that demonstrates key concepts of human anatomy and physiology, particularly those related to the digestive system and nutrient metabolism.

The project translates biological principles into a mechanical simulation model called the Metabolic Maze, where nutrients travel through a sequence of stations representing physiological processes in digestion, absorption, and nutrient regulation.

Instead of constructing a physical model, this project presents the simulation through a digital website that visually demonstrates how different nutrients are processed in the body and how biological systems interact to maintain metabolic balance.

The website contains seven stations, each representing a biological mechanism involved in digestion and nutrient processing.

Project Objective

The purpose of this project is to demonstrate the integrated principles of animal anatomy and physiology through a digital simulation. The website aims to simulate digestive system processes, show how nutrients are processed, stored, absorbed, or eliminated, illustrate the mechanical logic behind physiological functions, and provide an interactive educational experience through a digital platform.

Website Features

The website contains seven stations representing different biological processes in the digestive system.

Station 1: Herbivore Intake

This station simulates dietary selection in herbivorous organisms.

Concept Demonstrated
Herbivores primarily consume plant-based materials. Non-compatible food types are rejected by the intake system.

Simulation
The intake funnel accepts plant-like nutrient tokens such as nuts, leaves, and berries while rejecting incompatible food items such as animal-based materials or non-edible objects.

Station 2: Vitamin Partition

This station demonstrates the difference between fat-soluble and water-soluble vitamins.

Concept Demonstrated
Water-soluble vitamins dissolve in water and are easily excreted from the body. Fat-soluble vitamins are stored in body tissues for long-term use.

Simulation
Water-soluble vitamins are directed into a waste pathway. Fat-soluble vitamins such as A, D, and K are diverted into a storage tank representing long-term tissue storage.

Station 3: Nutritional Stress Test

This station illustrates the effects of nutrient deficiency and insufficient energy intake.

Skeletal Support
A structural pillar labeled Phosphorus supports the system.

Concept Demonstrated
Minerals such as phosphorus are essential for skeletal strength and structural integrity. Removing the mineral support demonstrates how deficiency can lead to structural instability.

Energy Gate
A weighted gate only opens when enough nutrients enter the system.

Concept Demonstrated
Adequate energy intake is necessary for metabolic processes to continue. If insufficient nutrients are present, the gate remains closed and metabolic activity stops.

Station 4: Pepsin pH Gate

This station simulates the pH changes between the stomach and the small intestine.

Concept Demonstrated
Pepsin functions in the highly acidic environment of the stomach. When food moves into the small intestine, pancreatic secretions neutralize the pH, stopping pepsin activity.

Simulation
Protein particles are released only when the acidic stomach condition is triggered. Once the particles move into the intestinal section, the pathway changes to represent enzyme inactivity under neutral pH conditions.

Station 5: Nutrient Divergence

This station demonstrates the unique absorption pathway of lipids compared to other nutrients.

Concept Demonstrated
Lipids are absorbed into the lymphatic system through structures called lacteals, while carbohydrates and proteins enter blood capillaries.

Simulation
Lipid tokens move into a separate lacteal or lymphatic pathway, while carbohydrates and proteins move into a capillary pathway representing entry into the bloodstream.

Station 6: Microbiome Blockade

This station represents the role of intestinal bacteria in the large intestine.

Concept Demonstrated
Gut microbiota assist in digestion and regulate water absorption. Disruption of these bacteria, such as through antibiotics, can impair normal intestinal function.

Simulation
A removable barrier labeled Bacterial Flora regulates the flow of materials through the system. When the barrier is removed, water moves through the system too rapidly to be properly reabsorbed.

Station 7: Comparative Adaptor

This station demonstrates anatomical differences between the digestive systems of two birds: a sparrow and a hawk.

Sparrow Setup
The sparrow configuration includes a gizzard module that contains grinding materials such as pebbles or sand. It also uses a longer digestive pathway to process seeds and grains.

Hawk Setup
The hawk configuration bypasses or removes the gizzard and uses a shorter digestive pathway suited for a carnivorous diet.

This comparison shows how digestive anatomy adapts to different dietary requirements.

How to Use the Website

Step 1
Open the website in a modern web browser.

Step 2
Begin at Station 1, the Herbivore Intake.

Step 3
Follow the nutrient pathway as it moves through each station.

Step 4
Observe how the simulation demonstrates the biological process at each stage.

Step 5
Use the interactive or visual elements to understand how nutrients move through the metabolic system.

Step 6
Continue through all stations until reaching Station 7, where comparative digestive adaptations are demonstrated.

Proof of Function Demonstration

The project includes a short video demonstration showing the movement of nutrients through the metabolic maze. The demonstration highlights the vitamin partition system, lipid divergence into the lymphatic pathway, and the effects of removing the phosphorus structural support. During the demonstration, the biological reasoning behind each mechanism is explained.

Educational Value

This project helps students understand digestive physiology, nutrient metabolism, enzyme activity and pH dependence, vitamin storage and excretion, lymphatic versus circulatory nutrient transport, microbiome function, and evolutionary digestive adaptations.
